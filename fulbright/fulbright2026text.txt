ANSWER: 
My love of biology spurred my career in the sciences, but my path is guided by my love of other cultures and languages. This intersection is particularly clear for my research on winegrapes. 

I became fascinated by winegrapes 15 years ago, when a colleague told me they were the most diverse plant in terms of their seasonal develeopment. The variety Pinot noir generally flowers 3-4 weeks before Cabernet-Sauvignon, and these two varieties represent just a fraction of the total diversity grown on earth. This diversity, however, is still mostly in the ancestral home of winegrapes---Europe plants over 1000 different varieties of winegrapes, while the rest of the world plants a couple hundred. Thus, working in Europe is central to the my research aims. 

Years ago, when I wanted to find out the scope of winegrape seasonal development diversity I needed to meet experts on the topic in France. I also wanted to be sensitive to their culture. France's government research gardens protect and preserve thousands of varieties and wild grape species. They also help preserve the heritage and culture of viticulture in France, and they want to see it appreciated and protected. 

Before meeting any French winegrape researchers I spent two months living with a host family and studying French at the Alliance Francaise in 2011. I was an NSF postdoc at the time and so spent several afternoons each week in the médiathèque writing papers, but I filled my days otherwise with the language and the culture. I bought a bicycle and traveled around town and the region, observing, interacting and engaging. Certainly I had days I was horribly lost or confused by the culture, but this type of frustration was not new to me and I knew I could push through it for great gains. Not surprisingly, I wrote my most cited paper while in France (Wolkovich et al. 2012, Nature) and I credit that in part to the benefit of doing research alongside learning a new language and culture. 

In October 2011, before returning to the US, I traveled to Bordeaux to meet Dr. Kees van Leeuwen, an expert in winegrapes and climate change. He had never met or heard of me, but picked me up the train station in Libourne and answered all my questions. At the end, he encouraged my research interests in winegrape diversity, introduced to me to colleagues in Bordeaux, Avignon and Montpellier (I also intend to meet to engage with colleagues in Bordeaux and Avignon, but I plan to stay in Montpellier, where the other researchers are concentrated). 

Since then I have visited France for a couple weeks most years to meet with colleagues in Bordeaux, Avignon, Montpellier and Sète. This constant visiting has helped develop collaborations, but I believe requires greater immersion to make major research leaps. As such I am applying for a Fulbright to spend 6 months in France.

My research is guided by the critical question: How do communities dis-assemble and assemble with global change? My long-term aim is to provide robust predictions for wild and agricultural plant communities. My proposed work here focuses on improving our models of temperature response, which determine heat tolerance and thus the basis of when to change cultivars (and eventually crop species) with warming.

I started my career focused on human-altered space. I studied spatial ecology, a discipline charged with predicting the consequences of habitat loss and fragmentation. I worked in San Diego, where a system of canyons too steep to build on and parks provided an ideal test system of `islands' and `mainlands' of habitat for my work on plant invasions. By the end of my PhD, however, I had become more focused on human-altered time. I found the biggest impacts of invasive plants came through their timing, and how it was warped by climate change. 

After my PhD I thus focused on phenology---the timing of recurring life history events. I found that across systems non-native and invasive species from the northeast to Central Plains of North America had distinct phenologies from native species, yet ecology lacked a fundamental understanding of how plant phenology affects community structure. Since then I have focused on developing such a predictive framework.

ANSWER:
I grew up in a small rural town in New Hampshire, which I moved to when I was six. I was raised almost entirely by my mother who filled our home with books that were full of the words, images, joys and challenges of other countries, cultures, and perspectives. Before New Hampshire, my family had lived outside of Boston. Because of this, many people in our town considered us outsiders, especially compared to those who had lived there for generations, but my world at home helped me see the gifts of our new town, how to adapt to it, and some of its challenges. For example, while most students in my town did not attend college and my parents also had not (my Mum attended college when I did), I always assumed that I would.

These struggles growing up prepared me in part for the challenges of adapting to new cultures: the openness to embrace differences, to work through frustrations and see the benefits. I built on these skills over my career and am sure I will leverage them, while developing new approaches if selected for this Fulbright. 

In college I majored in biology, but took extensive classes in Spanish and Latin American literature, studying abroad in Oaxaca, Mexico where I became fluent in Spanish, and minored in Russian---a new language to me, but one that fascinated me, and a one I spoke quite well when I finished college. In my last year of college, I spent a month at Lake Baikal and helped translate for biologists on the trip. During my PhD I taught abroad--twice--on the foreign studies program centered in Costa Rica and during my postdoc when I became interested in winegrapes I studied French in Toulouse for two months. Since 2019 (with a Covid interruption) I have taught biology for several weeks in Namibia. 

I expect my biggest challenge in France will be the language. French will be the main language of almost everyone around me and I do not want my colleagues to adapt for me. As such, I have already spent one month this year in immersion classes in France and am taking regular lessons now in Vancouver. While I currently speak enough French to join coffee hours, I know how much further my collaborations will go when I am fluent enough to speak French with my colleagues all day.
